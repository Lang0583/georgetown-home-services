Summer Plumbing Checklist

Georgetown, TX homeowners — tick these off before peak heat and drought-season water use.

Local context: Hard water is common in Georgetown, which wears through cartridges, valve seats, and heater elements faster than in soft-water regions. Attics and exterior walls see brutal afternoon sun—pipes and hose bibs expand and stress seal points. High irrigation demand can mask creeping leaks until your bill or foundation tells you. Use this list in late spring so small problems don’t become Labor Day emergencies.
  1. 1. Water heater efficiency & safe operation

    Check the temperature is set to a safe, efficient range (many homes use ~120°F). Note any moisture, rust, or rumbling; flush sediment per manufacturer guidance if you’re comfortable—otherwise schedule a pro before summer guests increase hot-water load.

  2. 2. Outdoor hose bibs & irrigation tie-ins

    Exercise each outdoor spigot; look for drips at the wall, cracked vacuum breakers, or hoses left pressurized in sun. Replace worn washers; note any leak that continues when the valve is fully off—frost-free bibs can fail at the stem inside the wall.

  3. 3. Leak detection (bills, meters & quiet listening)

    Read your water meter, avoid water use for an hour, then re-read—movement suggests a leak. Check toilets for phantom fills, under-sink traps for moisture, and the water heater pan. Spikes on your Georgetown utility bill without lifestyle changes warrant a closer look.

  4. 4. Pipe insulation in extreme heat & UV

    Inspect foam or rubber insulation on attic supply lines and outdoor-exposed pipes; replace UV-brittle or animal-damaged sections. In hot attics, cold lines can sweat—proper insulation reduces condensation that stains ceilings and grows mold.

  5. 5. Water pressure spot-check

    If you have a gauge, note static pressure (often ~40–80 psi is typical for residential—verify against local norms). Hammering pipes, seeping T&P valves, or failing fixture cartridges can signal pressure that’s too high; chronic low pressure may mean obstruction or utility-side issues—document before calling a plumber.

  6. 6. Main shutoff & angle stops

    Locate the main house shutoff and confirm it turns (don’t force a seized valve). Test key angle stops under sinks and toilets so they’re not stuck open—summer travel is easier when you can isolate water quickly.

  7. 7. Drains before heavier use

    Run water in guest baths and rarely used sinks; slow gurgles may mean developing clogs. Avoid chemical drain products on whole-house slowdowns—multiple fixtures acting up can mean a main-line issue worth a camera or cabling pro.

  8. 8. Slab & foundation vigilance

    Note new warm floor strips, unexplained humidity, or carpet dampness along walls—possible slab or wall leak in our expansive-clay zone. Pair visual checks with your meter test; unexplained usage plus soil movement around Georgetown merits a licensed plumber, not guesswork.
